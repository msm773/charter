# When Software Moves Faster Than Business

## AI agents are changing how quickly we build software. But what happens when the rest of the organization can't keep up?
{: .article-subtitle}

![2016 and 2026 office and golf course illustration showing AI agents changing the bottleneck in software delivery]({{ '/assets/images/articles/when-software-moves-faster-than-business.webp' | relative_url }})

Given the right instructions and specifications, AI agents can sometimes build working software faster than a trip to the break room for coffee and a quick chat.

Sure, there are questions about quality and reliability. But there's no denying that agents are changing how software gets written, and we're still early in their evolution.

Which raises an interesting question:

**If agents make software implementation faster, cheaper, and easier, what does that mean for the business?**

Will businesses deliver more value, or simply produce more software?

Will they become more agile, making better decisions faster?

Or could an abundance of possibilities and information actually make those decisions harder?

I've been thinking about these questions while working with AI agents. One does have a little more time to ponder such things while the agents churn away at the code.

## When Implementation Becomes Abundant

When people outside of tech ask what AI means for business productivity, I like to say:

**AI might make an individual 10 times more productive, but that doesn't mean the company becomes 10 times more productive.**

This usually earns me a blank stare. What do you mean?

Give a capable agent a well-defined task, a good specification, and a way to verify its work, and the results can be impressive.

But building an entire product is a different proposition. Someone still needs to decide what to build, whether customers actually want it, and whether it makes business sense.

So even when the agents are ready to build, the business may not be ready to decide.

What happens when implementation suddenly becomes dramatically faster than everything around it?

Here's a hypothetical example of weekly capacity:

| Activity | Before AI | With AI | Improvement |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Product decisions finalized | 5 | 7 | 1.4× |
| Features implemented | 5 | 40 | 8× |
| Features independently verified | 5 | 12 | 2.4× |
| Features approved for release | 5 | 8 | 1.6× |

*Illustrative capacities, not measured industry results.*

Suppose each feature needs a business decision before development can proceed.

In this simplified example, the business can finalize only seven feature decisions per week, limiting delivery to seven features: a 40% improvement.

The agents might have the capacity to implement forty features, but there isn't enough approved work to take full advantage of that capacity.

The principle remains: accelerating one part of a system doesn't automatically accelerate the whole system.

And unlike an implementation bottleneck, some business constraints may not have a technical solution.

## When Business Can't Move at Machine Speed

So why not use AI to accelerate the rest of the business?

Give everyone an agent and let them get to work!

But business decisions aren't always slow because an organization is inefficient.

Perhaps they're waiting for quarterly sales results before deciding which product to expand. Maybe customer research hasn't established whether a proposed feature is worth building. Or they're entering a new market, but can't launch until the necessary approvals are in place.

Unfortunately, giving everyone an AI agent doesn't make quarterly sales results arrive sooner, or regulatory approvals materialize overnight.

Customers need time to use products. Markets take time to reveal themselves. And strategic decisions often involve risks and tradeoffs that can't simply be automated away.

Sometimes waiting is exactly the right thing to do.

A software feature delivered in two hours instead of two weeks doesn't necessarily change whether the company should release it.

But not every business decision depends on waiting for something outside our control.

Some decisions are difficult because exploring the alternatives has traditionally been expensive.

And that's where faster, cheaper software implementation could change things.

## Rethinking How We Make Decisions and Build Software

Take product development, for example.

Instead of spending weeks debating which of three proposed features to build, imagine spinning up three agents in the morning and comparing the rough prototypes that afternoon!

Not every product problem will cooperate, of course. A prototype doesn't tell us whether customers will buy something, whether a market is ready, or whether a product is commercially viable.

But it can give us something tangible to evaluate much earlier, helping us make better-informed decisions about what to build.

AI changes the economics of experimentation. Ideas that previously required significant time and money to explore can now be turned into working prototypes much more easily.

Suddenly, we can afford to experiment with ideas that might never have made it past the conference room.

That's a rather interesting possibility.

Of course, if we can produce three prototypes in an afternoon, why not ten? We may find ourselves with more possibilities than we can reasonably evaluate.

More information doesn't automatically produce better decisions. Sometimes it just produces more meetings, more discussions, and more indecision.

**And business decision-making isn't the only thing likely to change. The way we develop software may need to evolve as well.**

After all, writing code is only one part of developing a reliable product. There's architecture, design, integration, testing, security, release management, and the ongoing challenge of keeping a complex system understandable.

If implementation becomes dramatically faster, the balance among those activities changes too.

Consider quality assurance.

In my own work with agents, I've had multiple agents perform different types of testing overnight, leaving me a list of defects to investigate in the morning.

With agents handling more of the testing, practices like continuous verification may become easier to incorporate throughout development.

**Which brings us to the software development lifecycle.**

We've spent decades refining software development methodologies like Waterfall and Agile to help coordinate people, manage uncertainty, maintain quality, and deliver software.

How many of our existing practices are shaped by the fact that writing software has traditionally been expensive and time-consuming?

And how many exist because developing the *right* software is inherently difficult?

Those seem like increasingly important distinctions.

Maybe AI will lead us toward entirely new development methodologies. Or perhaps it will make some longstanding principles far more practical: rapid iteration, continuous feedback, automated verification, and closer collaboration.

## So, Where Does That Leave Us?

Businesses still operate in a world of customers, competitors, budgets, regulations, uncertainty, and decisions that often have little to do with technology.

Making software easier to build doesn't necessarily make any of those things move faster.

And perhaps it shouldn't.

What I find particularly interesting is that faster, cheaper software development may change more than how quickly we produce software.

It may change how we explore ideas, evaluate alternatives, and decide what is worth building in the first place.

Some organizations may use that capability to deliver more software. Others may use it to experiment more freely, make better-informed decisions, or reconsider how their development processes work.

And some may discover that the most important constraints were never about writing software at all.

I don't know how this will play out. And I'm not convinced anyone has definitive answers just yet.

But making software dramatically easier to build is an extraordinary achievement.

**Figuring out how to turn that capability into better products, better decisions, and greater business value may be an equally interesting challenge.**

*I'd love to hear what others are experiencing. Is AI primarily accelerating your existing development processes, or is it beginning to change how your organization decides what to build in the first place?*
